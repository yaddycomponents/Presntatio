A platform modernization, in four acts, beginning with the trouble.

Faster, lighter, built to scale. Those were the instructions, and this is what happened.

Act One, in which a decade of good news quietly becomes heavy.

Thirteen megabytes on every visit, six seconds of waiting, and fifteen filter systems that all disagreed.

Act Two, in which they begin, as one does, by deleting things.

Two hundred twenty-nine commits, one thousand seven hundred sixty-eight files, and rather more deleted than written.

Shipped as one enormous parcel, it was opened, sorted, and repacked into two hundred forty-nine smaller ones.

Here is where the weight had been living: four bundles, comfortable and undisturbed, downloaded by everyone.

The dashboard used to ask for everything at once. Now it asks only for what a dashboard needs.

Without preload the browser finds its files one by one; with it, all at once, and the page begins sooner.

The pointer rests on Inbox without clicking, and the page's bundles begin arriving quietly in the background — assets only, no requests to the server, nothing yet decided.

One function, three hundred fifty-seven kilobytes, delivered faithfully on every page load to people who never used it.

The remedy was a single line, and three hundred fifty-seven kilobytes became nothing at all.

Time to paint, halved. The screen now arrives before you have finished deciding to wait.

For years the build needed twelve gigabytes of heap and a ten-gigabyte swap file to keep from collapsing. Rolldown builds in Rust, off the heap entirely, so both were simply deleted.

Certain lessons were written down, so they would not have to be learned twice.

The styles had been living inside the JavaScript, recomputed on every render. They live in ordinary files now, computed once, by the build.

Cascade layers decide who wins, and ours are ordered so the last one does. Then version six arrived with unlayered styles, sitting above them all. This was handled.

There had been fifteen filter systems, so every fix had to be made fifteen times. They are now a single configuration-driven module.

One filter now — the same pills, the same saved views, across fifteen pages that never once agreed.

It can be driven entirely from the keyboard, which the people who live in it all day notice first.

Architecture that compounds: each thing built upon it costs less than the thing before.

Every tile used to fetch at once, looked at or not. Now a tile fetches when it comes into view.

The list renders only what is on screen — a thousand rows, or ten, and the browser cannot tell the difference.

Your place is kept in the address bar: the filters, the page, the customer. Send a colleague a link, and they see precisely what you saw.

React eighteen, Vite eight, React Query — the foundation replaced while the building stayed open.

Four leaks were found in the startup path, objects made on every render and never once closed.

Act Three, in which we total up what all of it actually bought.

The dashboard loads in seven hundred fifty-five milliseconds, two and a half megabytes, on fourteen startup calls where there were once forty-three.

Every line moves the same direction, which is rarer than it sounds and was not guaranteed.

Two thirds less code was written, and compression did the remaining third. We cite bytes over the wire, because those are the ones you wait for.

The build once took one minute forty-four. It now takes fifteen seconds — ninety seconds returned on every push, to every engineer, forever.

A word of honesty: different deployments, different datasets, cold loads. Read the direction, and not the decimals.

One filter system, not fifteen. If you remember one line, we would prefer it were that one.

It loads two and a half times faster, and the feeds no longer freeze. Nobody had to be told.

One toolkit, less code to own, and a team now spending its time on things that did not exist last year.

Act Four, in which a faster build invents an entirely new kind of mistake.

The library now ships its own expertise. Alfred keeps the house and writes nothing himself; he decides who should. Real work is sent away, and reports back.

Neo reads every pull request first. On this one it found a command-injection hole in the workflow written to harden the pipeline — one dollar seventy-six, seven minutes, no human involved.

The platform, re-engineered: faster, lighter, and considerably harder to break. Thank you for your attention.
